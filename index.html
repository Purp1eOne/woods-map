    Routes, lines & areas

      Measure & walking time

      This is a straight line, not a trail route. Real walking through brush, water or hills usually takes longer.

      How to use Woods Map

      First time

      Open the app on wifi and allow location when it asks. Tap Locate at the bottom left. The blue dot is you. The pale circle around it shows how accurate the GPS is. It is smaller in the open than in thick woods.

      Maps and layers

      Tap Layers at the top right and pick Topo (contour lines), Satellite, Satellite + trails, Hybrid or Street. Satellite + trails is the clear satellite photo with the Topo map's trails drawn in orange. Hybrid adds road and trail names and faint contour lines, but it shades public land such as wildlife refuges a pale white. Both have the same trails as Topo, not every path in the woods. Switch on MN DNR public land to see Minnesota Wildlife Management Areas, or MN walk-in access for southwest Minnesota. Parcel lines (Becker, Otter Tail) draws property lines in yellow once you zoom in close. Tap inside a parcel to see the owner, acres and legal description from county tax records. The tax year shows how current that record is. Some Becker parcels are several years old, so check the county's own parcel search if the owner looks wrong. Parcel lines are approximate and are not a survey. Lidar shows the bare ground in fine detail (see Lidar maps below). The map covers Minnesota and Illinois; Illinois has the same Topo, Satellite and Street maps but no public-land overlay yet. Street needs a signal. Topo, Satellite, Satellite + trails and Hybrid can be saved for use offline. Satellite + trails takes about twice the space, since it saves two sets of images.

      Adding pins

      • Pin me drops a pin where you are standing.
      • Pin center drops a pin at the crosshair. Pan and zoom the map first. This is how to mark a spot you are not standing on. The numbers at the top left are the crosshair position.
      • Pressing and holding on the map also starts a pin.
      • To mark something you can see but aren't standing on, such as a deer you ranged, use Mark in compass mode (see Compass mode below).

      Give it a name, a type and notes. There are about 25 types, grouped into stands, deer sign (scrapes, rubs, beds, trails, funnels, sightings, sheds), food and water, turkeys and ducks, and access (truck, gate, entry route, property corner, hazard). Pick a color too, or leave it on the type's own color. Colors are handy for sorting spots by year or by who found them. Tap Add photo to take or attach a picture, such as a trail cam shot or a big rub. A range ring draws a 30 to 200 yard circle around the pin. Trail cameras and harvests also keep a date, and cameras show how long ago you checked them.

      Routes

      Open Pins and tap Plan a route. Tap the map where you want to start, then tap each place along the way. With Follow trails & roads on, each leg follows trails and roads from OpenStreetMap, which needs a signal. Trails that aren't in OpenStreetMap won't snap, and where nothing connects your points you get a straight line. Turn it off to draw straight legs through the woods. You can mix both in one route.

      A route shows its distance, its walking time (with extra for climbing), and how much it climbs and drops, from USGS elevation maps. Open it to see the elevation profile. Plan the walk to your stand in daylight, then use Follow in the dark.

      Following a route, line or walk

      Tap a route or line and choose Follow, or a walk's Follow back. The yellow bar at the top shows how far is left, the walking time and which way to head. If you drift more than about 40 yards off, it says how far off you are and points you back, with a dashed line to the route. Lines and routes take you to whichever end is farther from you. Follow back takes a walk back to where it started. Tap ✕ to stop.

      Lines and areas

      Open Pins and tap Draw a line or Draw an area. Then tap the map to add points. You can also line up the crosshair and tap ✚ Crosshair, which is easier to place exactly. Drag a point to move it, and Undo takes back the last one. A line shows its length and walking time, which is handy for entry routes, deer trails and property lines. An area shows its acres and the distance around it, for bedding areas, food plots, CRP or the land you have permission on. While you draw, each leg of a line and each side of an area shows its own length. Tap a saved line or area to see them again. Tap Done, or for an area tap its first point. Then give it a name, color, group and notes.

      Tap a line or area on the map to edit it, move its points, share it or hide it. They're listed under the pins in Pins, and Show on map can hide all lines or all areas. Share sends a KML file that Woods Map, Google Earth, onX and most map apps can open.

      Using your pins

      Tap a pin on the map for Go there, Edit, Wind, Share and Export. Wind loads the hourly wind forecast for the pin and draws an orange cone showing where your scent drifts. Tap an hour to see how it changes, which helps pick the stand for the day. It needs a signal. Export saves just that pin as a Woods Map file (JSON, with its photo), which you or a friend can load with Import pins. Share sends the pin by text, email or any app, with its photo if it has one. The message has a Google Maps link and a Woods Map link. A friend with Woods Map who opens that link gets the pin ready to save, in a group called "Shared with me". Tap a pin's photo to see it full screen. The Pins button lists every pin, nearest first once GPS is on. Sort can list them newest, oldest, by name or by type instead. You can search, or tap Edit on any row to change or delete a pin.

      Groups and hiding pins

      Every pin, line and area belongs to a group, like a folder. Your own pins start in My pins. Type a new name in the pin editor's Group box to start another group, such as "2025 season". In Pins, open Show on map to hide or show any mix of pin types, to show only pins of some colors or only pins with a photo, and to switch whole groups off or on. To hide just one pin, tap it and choose Hide, or tick Hide this pin on the map in the editor. It stays in the Pins list, greyed out, and tapping it there shows it again. Lines and areas hide the same way, from their own Hide button or editor. Hidden pins aren't deleted, and the Truck button skips them. A yellow chip at the top tells you when pins are hidden. Tap it to show everything again.

      Finding your way

      • Go there shows a yellow bar with distance, walking time and compass direction, plus a dashed line on the map. Tap the ✕ to stop.
      • The Truck button starts Go there to your nearest pin of type Parking / truck.
      • In Pins, Measure gives distance and time between any two points.
      • Compass on the right turns the map to face the way you do. See Compass mode below.
      • 3D on the right shows the land in 3D, to read ridges, saddles and draws. See 3D map below.

      Times are straight-line at the pace you set under More. Brush, water and hills make a real walk longer, so use a slower pace for rough ground.

      Compass mode

      Compass mode turns the map with your phone, so whatever is in front of you is at the top of the screen. Use it to line up the map with the woods, check which way a ridge or trail runs, or mark where you saw a deer.

      1. Tap Compass on the right side (the 🧭 button). It turns green while it's on. On an iPhone, the first time, tap Allow when it asks about Motion & Orientation.
      2. Hold the phone flat in front of you, like you're reading a map, and turn your body. The map turns with you.
      3. To go back to the normal map with north at the top, tap Compass again.

      What you see:

      • The blue beam from your dot is the direction the top of the phone is pointing. The blue fan shows roughly what's in front of you.
      • The white dashed rings are distances from you in yards, labeled along the beam. They change as you zoom: zoomed in they're 10 to 50 yards apart, zoomed out 100 yards or more.
      • The strip at the top slides like a real compass. The big number is your bearing in degrees, with the direction (for example 285° WNW). N is in red.

      The map stays centered on you and follows you as you walk, so you can't drag it. Pinch to zoom in or out. Pins, lines, routes and walks still show, and you can tap them. The Layers button is hidden while compass mode is on, so pick your map first.

      Mark a spot with a rangefinder (the bar above the bottom buttons):

      1. Range the deer, rub or tree with your rangefinder.
      2. Type that distance in the yards box, or use − and +.
      3. Point the top of the phone straight at it. Check that the blue beam runs toward it.
      4. Tap 📍 Mark. A new pin opens, that many yards out along the beam. It starts as a Buck sighting, with the distance and bearing in the notes. Change the name or type and tap Save.

      Later, use Go there on that pin to walk straight to the spot, for example to find blood or a downed deer. The app remembers the last distance you used. Your own GPS can be off by 10 to 30 yards in the woods, so the pin will be too.

      If the bearing looks wrong:

      • Move the phone in a slow figure 8 a few times to recalibrate. If the strip says move phone in a figure 8, the phone is asking for this.
      • Magnets and steel throw a compass off: magnetic phone cases and mounts, bino harness clips, a truck, a tree stand or a gun barrel. Step away and hold the phone flat.
      • Check it against a real compass now and then if you're relying on it.

      Bearings are magnetic, like a hand compass. In Minnesota that is within about 2° of true north. In Illinois it is about 3° to 4° west. Compass mode works with no signal, since it only uses the phone's own sensors and GPS. Some phones have no compass. On those the strip says No compass, and the map turns to the direction you're walking once you start moving.

      Lidar maps

      Lidar is elevation measured by laser from an airplane, accurate to a few inches and detailed to about 1 yard. Shaded, it shows the shape of the ground with the trees taken away: ditches, creek bottoms, benches, saddles, old logging roads and fence lines, small rises in a flat field, and the edges of swamps. That's where deer travel and bed, and most of it can't be seen on a topo map or satellite photo. It covers all of Minnesota and Illinois.

      There are two ways to see it. Tap Layers at the top right:

      • Lidar (shaded relief) is a map of its own: the bare ground in gray, lit from several sides. Flat ground is white and every slope, bank and ditch shows as a shadow.
      • Lidar shading (over any map) is a layer you switch on over Satellite, Topo or any other map. It darkens the slopes and leaves flat ground alone, so you see the ground's shape and the photo at once. It only shows once you zoom in past county level.

      Both work in the 3D view: pick Lidar in its map menu, or turn on the shading layer before opening 3D. Slopes are drawn steeper than they really are so small features stand out, so a gentle rise can look like a sharp bank. Check the topo lines or the 3D view for real steepness.

      USGS draws each lidar square on request, so it can take a few seconds to fill in, longer when their server is busy. Squares you've looked at are kept for next time. To use lidar with no signal, choose Lidar shaded relief under More > Offline maps. That download is slower than the other maps. The shading layer uses the same saved squares.

      3D map

      The 3D map shows the land in real hills and valleys, so ridges, saddles, benches, draws and funnels stand out. It's handy for planning a stand, an entry route or a stalk before you go.

      1. Move the 2D map to the spot you want, then tap 3D on the right (the ⛰ button). The first time it takes a few seconds to load.
      2. The map opens tilted. Drag with one finger to move around, and pinch to zoom.
      3. Tilt: put two fingers on the screen and slide them up or down together. Turn: twist two fingers. The arrow buttons at the top right also work. Tap the compass arrow there to face north again.
      4. Tap ✕ 2D at the top left to go back. The 2D map opens on the spot you were looking at.
      • Hills makes the land taller: 1× is true height, and 2× or 3× makes small rises easy to see. Most Minnesota and Illinois deer country is fairly flat, so 1.5× or 2× usually works best.
      • Shading adds shadows to the slopes, as if the sun were low in the northwest. That makes ridges and draws pop, even on the satellite map.
      • Look down flattens the view to straight overhead (and points north). Tap Tilt to tilt it again.
      • ◎ Me moves to where you are.
      • The menu at the top picks the map: Topo, Satellite, Satellite + trails, Hybrid or Street. The 2D map switches to match.

      Your pins, lines, areas, routes and walks all show in 3D, just as in 2D. Tap one for the usual buttons (Go there, Edit and so on), and press and hold the map to start a new pin. The public-land and parcel layers you have turned on in 2D also show. Pin types, groups or items you've hidden stay hidden.

      Hill heights come from USGS elevation data (about 10 m detail), so they show the shape of the land, not single trees or small ditches. To use 3D with no signal, save the area under More > Offline maps with Also save 3D terrain ticked. The 3D map needs a fairly recent phone; it works on most phones from the last several years.

      Recording your walk

      Tap Track, then Start recording. It records the path you walk so you can follow it back out. The screen stays on while it records, and it only records while the app is open. Pause stops counting while you sit, for example on stand. Saved walks show on the map as a dashed line and stay there until you hide them.

      Tap a walk in the list, or on the map, for its distance, total and moving time, average speed, climb and descent, and elevation profile. There you can rename it, pick a color, add notes, Follow back to the start, Share it as a GPX file, or Save as a route so you can follow the same path next time. ✂ Trim cuts a stretch off the start or end, for when you forgot to stop at the truck. If you stopped by mistake, open your newest walk and tap Keep recording (within 12 hours).

      Sunrise and shooting hours

      The chip at the top shows sunrise and sunset for where you are. Tap it for the big game / deer shooting window, which is 30 minutes before sunrise to 30 minutes after sunset in both Minnesota and Illinois. Other seasons have different hours, so check the current regulations.

      Insights: the rut and your deer seasons

      Tap Insights on the bottom bar for the whitetail rut phase on any date you pick: pre-rut, seeking, chasing, peak or post-rut. It also shows how long until the next phase, what deer are doing, and where to hunt. The peak, November 10 to 16, comes from Minnesota DNR breeding research. The other phases are estimates. Tap Why these dates? for the sources.

      Below the rut, Insights uses your GPS to find the Minnesota deer permit area you're in and shows its archery, firearms and muzzleloader dates, straight from the DNR's permit-area data. Each season shows whether it's open on the date you picked, and which rut phases it covers. Looking up a new area needs a signal, but the last one is saved for when you're out of service. Always confirm with the current DNR regulations.

      Coordinates

      Tap the coordinates at the top left to copy them, or to type in a spot and jump there or pin it. It reads 46.3045, -94.2001 and also N 46° 18.270 W 94° 12.006.

      Before you leave: save maps for offline

      1. Do this on wifi. Pan and zoom so the screen covers the area you hunt.
      2. Tap More, then Offline maps. Choose a map and a detail level, then Download this area.
      3. Leave Also save 3D terrain ticked if you want the 3D map to work out there.
      4. Repeat for the other map if you want both.
      5. Turn on airplane mode and check that the map still shows.

      Your pins, tracks and GPS dot work with no signal. Saved maps live on your own phone, so each person downloads their own.

      Backup and sharing

      Under More, Export pins (backup) saves a file of your pins, routes, lines, areas and walks, with their colors and photos. Import pins loads one (a Woods Map backup, GPX, or KML from Google Earth and other map apps, including their lines and areas) and never changes or removes your own pins. It asks which group to put the new pins in, such as "Mike's pins", so you can hide them all in one tap. Then it asks whether to show them on the map now. Choose Cancel to bring them in hidden and switch them on later in Pins > Show on map. When restoring your own backup, leave the group empty to keep your groups as they were. To share spots, send the file to a friend and have them import it. Export now and then, since pins live only on this phone.

      Good to know

      • GPS under thick cover or in a deep valley can be off by 20 to 30 yards or more. When you drop a pin, wait for the circle to shrink.
      • Public land boundaries are for reference. Private land is not shown. Confirm access rules and ownership before you hunt.
      • GPS and Track use extra battery, more so in the cold.
      • Pick Dark, Light or Match my phone under More.

      Insights

      Whitetail rut (Minnesota)

        A planning guide, not a forecast. Day length sets when does breed. Weather, the moon and hunting pressure change how much deer move in daylight, not when the rut happens.

        Why these dates?

        The peak is the best-supported part. The Minnesota DNR's White-tailed Deer Handbook (2000) puts the midpoint of the rut in the week of November 12, when about half of does 1½ years and older have been bred. Northern Minnesota research from 1983–87 (Fuller 1990, cited in a 2005 DNR report) estimated peak conception at November 10–14. The DNR says deer breed from November into early December.

        The other phase boundaries are estimates that split the season into the phases hunters use. They are not official DNR dates. Minnesota hasn't published separate breeding dates for the north and south, so the same calendar is used statewide.

        The second rut note comes from the handbook too: a doe that isn't bred comes back into heat about 28 days later, and some doe fawns breed in December.
